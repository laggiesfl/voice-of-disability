Our research foundations

From awareness to action

Why accessible information alone does not guarantee disability inclusion.

The research behind our approach

In her 2004 University of Liverpool MBA dissertation, Barriers to Gainful Employment for Disabled People in South Africa, Voice of Disability founder Fadila Lagadien examined obstacles that disabled jobseekers and employees encountered despite employment equity legislation.

Her qualitative study used semi-structured interviews with 20 participants and documentary research. She had expected reasonable accommodation to be the principal barrier. In the analysis, however, ignorance emerged as the main category, with perception of disability and commitment to change as its two subcategories.

This did not mean that reasonable accommodation was unimportant: the study also identified it as central to achieving equal opportunity. Rather, lack of understanding and commitment helped explain why accommodation and wider change were not being implemented effectively.

A lesson from her mother

In the dissertation, Fadila recalled a saying her mother used when someone claimed not to know something they could have learned: a person may refuse to hear or see what is available to them.

The original dissertation used historical expressions involving blindness and deafness. They were quoted as a family saying about wilful disregard for knowledge, not as descriptions of disabled people. We explain that context here rather than adopting disability-related terms as metaphors for unwillingness.

The central distinction: Information may be available, but inclusion depends on whether people with power are willing to use it and change their decisions, systems and practices.

Research findings and later experience

Fadila has described encountering similar patterns in her subsequent work with the SABC and on national structures and public- and private-sector boards.

These experiences help explain the continuing relevance of the dissertation. They are later professional observations, not additional interviews or results from the 2004 research sample.

A framework for learning and organisational change

We use this research to inform a proposed approach to disability inclusion learning and organisational assessment. The aim is not simply to increase awareness, but to translate what is learned into demonstrable change.

1. Knowledge

What barriers, rights and reasonable accommodations do decision-makers understand? Can they identify inaccessible practices?

2. Commitment

Will leaders question established assumptions, consult disabled people and allocate responsibility and resources?

3. Accountability

What barriers were actually removed? What evidence shows improved participation, access or opportunity?

Proposed learning activities: scenario-based reflection; an accessible barrier-identification exercise; a documented action commitment; and a follow-up review of evidence, including feedback from disabled people.

These are a proposed framework, not a claim that a fully evaluated training programme is already operating.

How this informs our work

Voice of Disability brings the voices, experiences and leadership of disabled women into advocacy, public dialogue and inclusive participation.

Organisational training and assessments can apply the knowledge–commitment–accountability framework to evaluate whether awareness leads to changes in practice. Any separate delivery arrangement with BeAccessible would need to be clearly identified and governed.

Research reference: Fadila E. Lagadien (2004), Barriers to Gainful Employment for Disabled People in South Africa, MBA dissertation, University of Liverpool. The findings reflect the study's time, sample and methods; they are not presented as current national statistics.
